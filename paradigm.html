The Healthcare Paradigm

Build the system around where healthcare happens.

Healthcare happens between people. The patient and care team are the center, where evidence, clinical judgment, individual circumstances, and available resources come together.

A dependable system organizes everything around that relationship to support timely, effective care.

01 / The destination

A system built from the center out.

The center is where care is planned, delivered, and refined. Each surrounding ring provides a function that makes this work more effective and timely. Beyond those rings, resource sectors contribute knowledge, tools, and funding. Their inputs inform care; they do not substitute for the judgment and relationship at the center.

A care-centered paradigm

Conceptual model, not an organizational chart. Resources and information also flow outward as care teams report needs, outcomes, and learning.

CENTERPatient + care team

Clinical judgment, shared decisions, treatment, continuity, and the patient's lived experience.

FIRST RINGDirect care support

Timely contact, follow-up, coaching, navigation, coordination, and appropriate integrative support.

SECOND RINGClinical access and resources

Diagnostics, specialists, therapies, medications, escalation, and urgent or hospital pathways.

THIRD RINGOperations and administration

Scheduling, records, staffing, communications, and dependable workflows.

FOURTH RINGFinancing, governance, and measurement

Financial protection, sustainable funding, accountability, shared standards, and oversight.

OUTER SECTORSSupply, evidence, and community resources

Research, technology, therapeutics, public infrastructure, and resource pools that enable care.

The purpose is not simply more encounters. It is more timely, appropriate contact, with continuity and escalation when people need it.
02 / The current paradigm

Many decision centers. Many delivery points. Too little connection.

Today's system has remarkable clinical capabilities, but independent decision structures can shape care through overlapping policies, interpretations, and constraints. At the delivery level, connections are often incomplete. Patients and care teams are left to bridge the gaps.

A fragmented, top-down structure

Illustrates common structural fragmentation, not every organization or patient experience. Existing integrated care teams demonstrate that stronger connections are possible.

03 / The transition

Change what the system is organized to do.

The path forward begins by strengthening the center, then aligning the surrounding resources. Better organization must improve both the effectiveness and the timeliness of care without shifting more burden onto patients.

01 / CONNECT

Establish continuity

Define a care team, its responsibilities, and a dependable way to maintain contact across encounters and settings.

02 / SUPPORT

Extend timely access

Use appropriate, more frequent support contacts and clear escalation pathways so scarce clinical expertise reaches people when needed.

03 / ALIGN

Make resources accountable to care

Test operating and financing arrangements, measure meaningful outcomes, refine them with patients and care teams, and expand what works.

The next question

How do we build it in Minnesota?

This paradigm provides a direction, not a claim that every answer is settled. The roadmap will identify what can be demonstrated, who must participate, how costs and risks can be shared, and what evidence would justify expanding the model.
